# Simple baselines vs complex models in dengue and other infectious-disease forecasting (persistence, seasonal naive, climatology, endemic channels, MEM), and the general forecasting evidence

Notes compiled 2026-10-07. Full texts were read via Europe PMC open-access XML where possible. Anything not verified from a primary source is listed under Gaps.

---

## Q1. Dengue Forecasting Project (Johansson et al. 2019 PNAS) and Johansson et al. 2016 (Sci Rep, Mexico): how did seasonal/historical baselines do against climate-driven and complex models?

### Takeaway
In both dengue benchmark studies a simple seasonal model built only from past dengue counts was very hard to beat. In Mexico, adding weather to SARIMA gave "generally very slight" gains, and a clear gain held up in only 1 of 17 states. In the 2019 multi-team challenge, a 4-5-parameter SARIMA baseline had the best calibration and the most skilful peak-week forecasts. Mechanistic models and models using climate data scored worse on average than purely statistical ones. Only the simple multi-model ensemble beat the uniform null model on every target.

### Cited Findings
**Johansson MA, Reich NG, Hota A, Brownstein JS, Santillana M. "Evaluating the performance of infectious disease forecasts: A comparison of climate-driven and seasonal dengue forecasts for Mexico." Sci Rep 2016;6:33707. doi:10.1038/srep33707**
- Design: monthly dengue cases in Mexico, Jan 1985 to Dec 2012 (28 years), at national level and for 17 endemic states. The data were split into training (1985–1989), evaluation (1990–2007) and a fully out-of-sample validation set (2008–2012). Horizons were 1–6 months. Metrics were MAE, R² and relative MAE. In all, 39 models were compared, "from the most simplistic, expecting future incidence to be the average of past incidence, to relatively complex seasonal autoregressive models incorporating weather covariates." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/) / [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep33707)
- There were two naive models: (i) the long-term historical mean and (ii) the historical mean for that calendar month ("seasonal"/climatology). "The model using the month of the year outperforms the long-term mean model by both MAE … and R²." "Despite being relatively naïve, predictions from the seasonal model have an R² of approximately 0.24." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Weather-only models: "The 4-month lag temperature model performed best, but did not outperform the simple monthly model by either metric." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Adding seasonal autocorrelation helped a lot: "the (1,0,0)(1,0,0)12 SAR model outperformed all of the previous models with lower MAE and higher R² at all prediction times." A first-order AR model beat the monthly model at 1–3 month horizons. — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Climate added little: "For some states, weather data slightly improved prediction at short time scales compared to the naïve seasonal model." "The improvements resulting from inclusion of weather variables were generally very slight, and only Jalisco had a clear improvement in relative accuracy in both the evaluation and validation datasets." In Michoacán, the locally selected temperature model "performed poorly in the out-of-sample validation period". Putting all weather variables into one model "did not yield noticeable improvements." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Accuracy ceiling: "At the 1 month horizon, R² for the optimal models rarely exceeded 0.8 and accuracy rapidly declined for longer horizons." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Recommendations: "models incorporating both autocorrelation and seasonality, such as the (1,0,0)(2,1,0)12 model, should always be used as a baseline for comparison." Studies should compare "to at least one baseline model that is not completely naïve." "Modeling efforts that leave out or combine the testing and validation components can lead to over-fitted models that perform poorly when used for prospective prediction." — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)

**Johansson MA, Apfeldorf KM, Dobson S, et al. "An open challenge to advance probabilistic forecasting for dengue epidemics." PNAS 2019;116(48):24268–24274. doi:10.1073/pnas.1909865116**
- Design: 16 teams; Iquitos, Peru (13 seasons of data) and San Juan, Puerto Rico (23 seasons). Three targets: peak incidence, peak week and season total. Forecasts were made every 4 weeks across 4 training seasons (2005/06–2008/09) and 4 testing seasons (2009/10–2012/13) and scored with the logarithmic score. 10 of the 16 teams (63%) used climate data. — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/) / [doi](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1909865116)
- Reference models: "a null model (equal probability assigned to each possible outcome), a baseline statistical time series model (a seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average [SARIMA] model), and a simple ensemble (an average of the probabilities of the 16 team and baseline forecasts)." — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- Results against baselines: "Many teams outscored both the null model and for each target except peak week, the baseline model." "The ensemble forecast outperformed most individual models and was the only forecast to outperform the null model for every target." — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- Early-season skill was poor: there was "low early season forecast skill, with many forecasts performing worse than a null forecast that assigned an equal probability to each possible outcome." Skill "was generally lowest for high incidence seasons, those for which forecasts would be most valuable." — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- Complexity hurt on average. Mechanistic approaches had lower log scores than purely statistical ones (−0.65, 95% CI −0.80 to −0.49). Ensemble approaches scored higher (+1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.13). Models using mechanistic approaches or climate data were less well calibrated. The authors wrote: "Our finding that statistical models generally outperformed mechanistic models is another indicator of the potential downside of overly complex forecasting models." — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- On the SARIMA baseline: "the relatively simple baseline SARIMA models (4 parameters for Iquitos, 5 for San Juan) … generally performed well compared with more complex models, including having the best overall calibration and the highest skill forecasts for peak week." On climate: "climate may determine dengue seasonality, but models characterizing seasonality using historical dengue data alone (e.g., the baseline SARIMA model) may be able to provide equivalent information." — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- Factors linked to higher scores: forecasts made later in the season (+0.043 per week), seasons with lower peaks (+0.43 per location-specific SD lower) and seasons with earlier peaks. Skill rose around the observed peak (median peak week 22.5 in San Juan, 28 in Iquitos). — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)

**Newer dengue evidence (for context)**
- Colón-González et al. 2021, PLoS Med (Vietnam superensemble, lead times up to 6 months): the superensemble "generated more accurate predictions than the baseline model 1 to 3 months ahead but not 4 to 6 months ahead". It did predict outbreaks better than the baseline at all lead times. — [PLoS Med doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1003542](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1003542) (from the search snippet; the baseline's exact definition was not checked)
- Infodengue-Mosqlimate Dengue Challenge 2024 (IMDC24, "Dengue Forecast Sprint"), PNAS 2026, doi:10.1073/pnas.2508989123. Six teams forecast weekly cases in 5 Brazilian states (AM, CE, GO, MG, PR). They trained on data to 2022 and tested on 2023, trained to 2023 and tested on 2024 (a record season, at the 100th percentile of historical peaks in MG, PR and GO), then made true forecasts for 2025. Models: LSTM, LSTM+Prophet, CNN-LSTM, Bayesian spatiotemporal, time-series decomposition and a "Bayesian baseline model". Results: "all models tended to overestimate the epidemic curve" in 2023. The best model differed by year and metric; the Bayesian baseline (M6) was among the top models in 2023 by CRPS around the peak and by log score in 2023–2024. "This performance variability highlighted the need for ensemble approaches." Brazilian authorities adopted the ensembles for 2025. — [PMC12912988](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12912988/) / [doi](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2508989123)

### Inferences
- Across dengue studies, most of the predictable signal comes from the seasonal climatology (month or week of year) plus recent autocorrelation. Climate covariates usually add only marginal skill, which is not reliably reproducible out of sample. This fits the idea that seasonality already encodes the average climate signal.
- The right baseline for a dengue forecaster is a SARIMA-type or "seasonal + AR" model, not just a uniform or mean model. Beating only a naive mean is weak evidence of skill.
- In the 2019 challenge, the ensemble beat the null model on every target while individual teams did not. This suggests the main gain from sophistication comes from combining models, not from any single complex model.

### Gaps
- I could not access the 2019 PNAS supplementary tables with per-team log scores against the SARIMA baseline, so I do not know exactly how many of the 16 teams beat the SARIMA on each target.
- The Colón-González 2021 baseline definition and skill numbers, and the 2025/2026 D-MOSS operational evaluation ([PLOS Glob Public Health](https://journals.plos.org/globalpublichealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgph.0005867)), were not read in full.

## Q2. FluSight, COVID-19 Forecast Hubs (Reich et al. 2019; Cramer et al. 2022; Bracher et al.; Lopez et al. 2024; Sherratt et al. 2023): how many models beat persistence or historical baselines at multi-week horizons, and what do ensembles add?

### Takeaway
Against a historical-average baseline (influenza) or a "same as last week" persistence baseline (COVID-19), about half to two-thirds of models win on average. The share that wins consistently falls sharply at 3–4 weeks for flu, and for COVID-19 cases often only 1–2 weeks of skill exist. The equal-weight ensemble is the only model that reliably beats the baseline everywhere. Persistence is especially strong at peaks and plateaus. Models do worst in growth and decline phases and tend to overshoot after turning points.

### Cited Findings
**Reich NG, Brooks LC, Fox SJ, et al. "A collaborative multiyear, multimodel assessment of seasonal influenza forecasting in the United States." PNAS 2019;116(8):3146–3154. doi:10.1073/pnas.1812594116**
- Baseline: ReichLab-KDE, which "forecasts the same historical average every week within a season and does not update based on recently observed data" (a climatology baseline). The study covered 22 models, 7 seasons and HHS regions, scored by log score expressed as probability. — [PMC6386665](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6386665/)
- "15 of 22 models outperformed the historical baseline in at least six of the seven seasons" for 1-week-ahead forecasts, but "only 7 of 22 models outperformed the historical baseline in at least six seasons when making 4-wk-ahead forecasts." — [PMC6386665](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6386665/)
- For seasonal targets, 16 of 22 models (73%) beat the historical average on average seasonal score. The baseline's average seasonal score was 0.20 (0.14 overall for seasonal targets; onset 0.11, peak week 0.17, peak intensity 0.13). The best model, LANL-DBM, scored 0.36, "more than a twofold increase". For week-ahead targets the baseline scored 0.12–0.37 depending on region. — [PMC6386665](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6386665/)

**Cramer EY, Ray EL, Lopez VK, et al. "Evaluation of individual and ensemble probabilistic forecasts of COVID-19 mortality in the United States." PNAS 2022;119(15):e2113561119. doi:10.1073/pnas.2113561119**
- Baseline: COVIDhub-baseline. Its "median predicted incidence [is] equal to the number of reported deaths in the most recent week", with uncertainty based on past weekly differences (a persistence/random-walk baseline). — [PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/)
- Coverage: 1–4 week-ahead state-level death forecasts over 79 weeks to 30 Oct 2021, in 50 states. Submissions rose from 4 models at the start to about 41.2 per week in 2021. — [PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/)
- Overall, "18 models had a relative WIS of less than 1 … and 10 models (including the baseline) had a relative WIS of 1 or greater". The abstract summary: "Two-thirds of the models evaluated showed better accuracy than a naïve baseline model." The ensemble's relative WIS was 0.61, meaning 39% less error than the baseline. — [PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/)
- By horizon: "11 models showed a lower average WIS (range: 24.9 to 34.3) than the baseline at a 1-wk horizon (average WIS = 35.8). At a 4-wk-ahead horizon, 19 models had a lower average WIS (range: 39.9 to 65.2) than baseline (average WIS = 70.1)." Persistence degrades faster with horizon for deaths, a lagging, trending signal. 4-week forecasts had about 76% more error than 1-week forecasts. — [PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/)
- The ensemble "was the only model that 1) outperformed the baseline forecast in every location, 2) had better overall 4-wk-ahead accuracy than the baseline forecast in every week, and 3) ranked in the top half of forecasts for more than 75% of the forecasts it made". Only the ensemble achieved near-nominal 50% and 95% interval coverage. More complex weighted ensembles showed "modest improvements in accuracy" but "undesirable increases in variability". Only 4 models beat the baseline at both 1 and 4 weeks in at least 3 of 4 pandemic phases. — [PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/)

**Lopez VK, Cramer EY, Pagano R, et al. "Challenges of COVID-19 Case Forecasting in the US, 2020–2021." PLoS Comput Biol 2024;20(5):e1011200. doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011200**
- Baseline: the most recent week's reported cases as the median for the next 4 weeks. "Seven of 22 team's forecast models outperformed the COVIDhub-baseline model at the state/territory/DC level … and 11 outperformed the baseline model at the national level." "Only two team forecasts (LNQ-ens1 and COVIDhub-4_week_ensemble) performed as well as or better than the baseline for all included states." "Few team forecasts outperformed a relatively simple and minimally informative baseline model." — [PMC11098513](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11098513/)
- By epidemic phase: "The baseline model performed relatively well in times of peaks and worse in nadirs … and in increasing and decreasing phases because it forecasted a flat trend." Model skill "was worst for the increasing phase followed by the decreasing phase". "Forecasts did not anticipate the speed of increase in cases in early winter 2020." — [PMC11098513](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11098513/)

**Bracher J, Wolffram D, Deuschel J, et al. "A pre-registered short-term forecasting study of COVID-19 in Germany and Poland during the second wave." Nat Commun 2021;12:5173. doi:10.1038/s41467-021-25207-0**
- Baselines: KIT-baseline (last observation carried forward), a multiplicative extrapolation from the last two points, and an ETS model. — [PMC8397791](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8397791/)
- "For deaths, a number of models were able to outperform a simple baseline forecast up to 4 weeks into the future, such improvements were limited to shorter horizons for cases." "For cases … almost none of the compared approaches meaningfully outperformed the naive baseline beyond a horizon of 1 or 2 weeks." The more complex extrapolation and ETS baselines "do not systematically outperform the naive baseline". — [PMC8397791](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8397791/)
- Lag and overshoot: model inertia "can lead to delayed adaptation to changes and explains why numerous models—including the ensemble—showed overshoot in the first half of November when cases started to plateau". Human-judgement forecasts "performed favourably immediately after changes in trends". — [PMC8397791](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8397791/)
- A companion paper covering Germany and Poland in early 2021 likewise reports that "many, though not all, models outperform a simple baseline model up to four weeks ahead". — [Bracher et al. 2022, Commun Med](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-022-00191-8)

**Sherratt K, Gruson H, Grah R, et al. "Predictive performance of multi-model ensemble forecasts of COVID-19 across European nations." eLife 2023;12:e81916. doi:10.7554/eLife.81916**
- Baseline: counts "stay the same as the latest data point over all future horizons, with expanding uncertainty". The ensemble's median relative WIS was 0.71 (IQR 0.61–0.88). The ensemble beat 83% of model forecasts for cases (N=886 predictions, 23 models) and 91% for deaths (N=763, 20 models). Covered 32 countries at 1–4 weeks. Ensemble performance "declined with longer forecast periods when forecasting cases, but remained stable over 4 weeks for incident death forecasts". The authors note "choice of baseline affects relative performance". — [PMC10238088](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10238088/) / [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/81916)

### Inferences
- How many models "beat the baseline" depends heavily on the baseline. Against climatology (flu KDE), a model only needs to use recent data. Against persistence (COVID), a model must forecast changes in trend correctly. Persistence is hard to beat at 1 week and at peaks and plateaus, and easier to beat at 3–4 weeks for steadily trending, lagged signals such as deaths.
- For dengue, which is strongly seasonal, the natural baselines are both persistence (short horizons) and seasonal climatology/SARIMA (longer horizons). A credible evaluation should report skill against both.
- Ensembles are the one robust "complex" improvement in every hub. Mainly they improve calibration and avoid catastrophic misses, rather than delivering large gains in point accuracy.

### Gaps
- The Bracher et al. 2021 PNAS comment on scoring (log score vs proper scores in FluSight) and Bracher et al. 2021 PLoS Comput Biol on interval scores were not read. The European Hub's per-model counts by horizon were not extracted.

## Q3. Endemic channels and outbreak thresholds for dengue (Bortman 1999, PAHO/WHO guidance, Brady et al. 2015, Badurdeen et al. 2013, Runge-Ranzinger et al. 2008, recent evaluations): definitions, sensitivity/specificity, and the problem of including outbreak years

### Takeaway
The endemic channel (Bortman 1999) builds expected weekly or monthly cases from 5–7 prior years: geometric mean with confidence limits, or median with quartiles. Thresholds are often set at mean + 2 SD. It is the operational standard across Latin America, but its parameters are rarely standardised or validated. Reported performance is modest: roughly 40% sensitivity and 90% specificity at +2 SD in Puerto Rico, and 66% sensitivity at +1 SD elsewhere. In Malaysia, the best Youden index was 0.26 for the conventional channel and 0.43 for a log-scale variant. Past epidemic years inflate the threshold, and choice of definition alone can change outbreak counts by up to 10 and the share of outbreak cases by up to 65 percentage points.

### Cited Findings
- **Bortman M. "Elaboración de corredores o canales endémicos mediante planillas de cálculo." Rev Panam Salud Publica 1999;5(1):1–8.** Shows how to build endemic channels in spreadsheets, using geometric means of rates and their standard deviation to set confidence intervals for expected and extreme values. — [PAHO IRIS](https://iris.paho.org/handle/10665.2/8562); [Dialnet](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=9555998)
- Common construction (Badurdeen et al. 2013): "Many countries use a version of the 'endemic channel' … based on the weekly (or monthly) average number of cases over the preceding 5 years. Above this is a line that represents +2SD; others use the median and the 3rd quartile." The zone between the mean and +2 SD is the "alert/alarm zone", and the zone above +2 SD or Q3 is the "epidemic zone". — [Badurdeen S, et al. BMC Public Health 2013;13:607, doi:10.1186/1471-2458-13-607](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3697990/)
- Country practice (10-country study across Latin America and Asia): "Only in 6 out of 10 countries was a fairly consistent definition applied". Colombia, Dominican Republic, Peru (partially) and Vietnam used +2 SD above the 5-year mean. Brazil and Malaysia used a "moving average", where the 4-weekly average exceeds the mean of three 4-weekly averages over the preceding 5 years. Brazil also used a threshold of more than 300 cases per 100,000. The time lag to response "seems to be usually above 2 weeks but often much longer". — [Badurdeen 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3697990/)
- Drawbacks listed by Badurdeen 2013: (a) managers treat crossing +2 SD as a warning when the outbreak is already underway, which delays response. (b) "Limited sensitivity (only 40% of such events when case numbers crossed the +2SD threshold were followed by a 'massive' increase of cases in Puerto Rico; using a similar predictor with a 1 SD threshold, Barbazan et al. found a sensitivity of 66%)." (c) "Outbreaks in previous years can result in thresholds that are too high; no satisfactory algorithm for recognising past aberrations has been devised." (d) An early seasonal rise can look like an outbreak. Weekly cases can cross +2 SD repeatedly in weeks 1–17 before a true take-off in week 18. — [Badurdeen 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3697990/). A 2026 paper cites the same Puerto Rico figures as about 40% sensitivity and 90% specificity at 2 SD, and 66% sensitivity in Brazil at 1 SD — [Umaña et al. 2026](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13155432/)
- **Brady OJ, Smith DL, Scott TW, Hay SI. "Dengue disease outbreak definitions are implicitly variable." Epidemics 2015;11:92–102. doi:10.1016/j.epidem.2015.03.002.** Tested 102 outbreak definitions from five endemic-channel types (recent mean, monthly mean, moving mean, cumulative mean, fixed incidence threshold of 100 or 300 per 100k). Parameters varied: ±outbreak-year trimming, 1 vs 2 SD, and 1–3 months above threshold. Data were Brazilian state-level dengue, evaluated annually for 2006–2013. Result: "Among the majority of definitions (95%), the total number of outbreaks identified by any given pair of definitions could differ by as many as 10 outbreaks and the total proportion of outbreak cases could differ by as much as 65% over just a nine-year time series." Thresholds are "often arbitrarily set at two standard deviations", and the +2 SD line "is exceeded frequently, briefly and sporadically". Countries adapt it, for example by requiring 2 successive weeks above threshold. — [PMC4429239](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4429239/)
- **Runge-Ranzinger S, Horstick O, Marx M, Kroeger A. "What does dengue disease surveillance contribute to predicting and detecting outbreaks and describing trends?" Trop Med Int Health 2008;13:1022–1041. doi:10.1111/j.1365-3156.2008.02112.x** is the systematic review cited by Badurdeen 2013 as foundational. — [reference list in PMC3697990](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3697990/); also Harrington J, Kroeger A, Runge-Ranzinger S, O'Dempsey T. J Trop Med 2013;2013:756832 (evaluation of country outbreak response plans), cited in [Umaña 2026](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13155432/)
- **Umaña JD, Montenegro-Torres J, Otero J, et al. "Endemic Channel Parametrization in Dengue Surveillance: Methodological Assessment of Retrospective Windows, Outbreak Trends, and Zero-Case Periods in Colombia." JMIR Public Health Surveill 2026;12:e79914. doi:10.2196/79914**
  - Practice is heterogeneous. In 2022, of 28 Colombian departments with dengue, only 12 showed an endemic channel. Of the 8 that stated a central measure, 12.5% used the mean, 12.5% the geometric mean, 37.5% the median and 37.5% more than one method. Window size was reported in only 16.7% of departments and ranged from 5 to 9 years. — [PMC13155432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13155432/)
  - Including vs excluding epidemic years: "no significant difference (P>.01) … for municipalities with high or very high transmission". In medium- and low-transmission units, performance changed by about +1.48% and −14.82%. In low-transmission municipalities, ignoring prior outbreak years was best. Replacing outbreak years with the geometric mean worked best in medium and very-high transmission. Excluding years retrospectively is criticised because "the selection uses the same criteria once the potential epidemic year has already occurred." — [PMC13155432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13155432/)
  - Shifting zero counts by 0.001 instead of +1 improved performance by an average of 23.07%. Shorter retrospective windows improved performance at all transmission levels. — [PMC13155432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13155432/)
- **Singh S, et al. "Optimizing Dengue Surveillance Thresholds in Malaysia: A Comparative Evaluation of Endemic Channel Approaches." Trop Med Infect Dis 2026;11(8):231. doi:10.3390/tropicalmed11080231.** Weekly channel built from a 3-year rolling baseline (EC = μ + mσ), with validation years 2017–2023 (2020–21 excluded from baselines). 260 weeks, of which 42 (16.2%) were outbreak weeks. Conventional SD channel at the classic m=2: sensitivity 0.26, specificity 0.89, PPV 0.27. Its best Youden index was 0.26 at m=1.0 (sensitivity 0.56, specificity 0.70, PPV 0.23). A log-scale channel with an enhanced alert rule reached Youden 0.43 at m=0.5 (sensitivity 0.60, specificity 0.82, PPV 0.35, NPV 0.93). The authors note that "unusually large outbreaks can inflate historical means and standard deviations, resulting in thresholds that become less responsive in subsequent years." — [PMC13517397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13517397/)
- **Moving Epidemic Method (MEM): Vega T, Lozano JE, Meerhoff T, et al. "Influenza surveillance in Europe: establishing epidemic thresholds by the Moving Epidemic Method." Influenza Other Respir Viruses 2013;7(4):546–558. doi:10.1111/j.1750-2659.2012.00422.x.** Overall sensitivity was 71.8% and specificity 95.5% (75.7% for ILI vs 60.1% for ARI countries), with median timeliness of 1 week (range 0–4.5). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1750-2659.2012.00422.x). MEM continues to be applied, for example to emergency-department data in NSW in 2026 — [Influenza Other Respir Viruses 2026, doi:10.1111/irv.70293](https://doi.org/10.1111/irv.70293)

### Inferences
- An endemic channel is effectively a seasonal-climatology baseline with an upper tolerance band. Its weaknesses mirror those of climatology forecasts: it has no persistence term, it reacts to past epidemics (inflated SD), and it is unreliable with few years or zero-heavy data.
- At the conventional +2 SD threshold, sensitivity is low (about 26–40% in the Malaysia and Puerto Rico data). Lowering the multiplier or log-transforming raises sensitivity at the cost of PPV. Any forecasting tool marketed as an improvement should be compared with an optimised endemic channel, not just the default +2 SD.

### Gaps
- The PAHO operational guidance document defining endemic channels for dengue (e.g., the IMS-dengue/EGI-dengue guidance) was not retrieved. Barbazan et al. and the original Puerto Rico 40% source (ref 13 in Badurdeen) were not read directly. No head-to-head dengue study of MEM vs endemic channel was found.

## Q4. General forecasting evidence: simple vs complex methods (M3/M4/M5, Makridakis et al. 2018 PLoS ONE, benchmarks, MASE, rolling-origin evaluation)

### Takeaway
On short or medium univariate series (about 14–126 observations), simple statistical methods and their combinations have repeatedly matched or beaten ML. In M3-based tests, every ML method lost to statistical methods at all horizons, and half of ML methods lost even to seasonal naive. ML won clearly only in M5, which had tens of thousands of related, long series with exogenous features. Best practice is to benchmark against naive/seasonal naive using scaled errors (MASE) and rolling-origin, out-of-sample evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- **Makridakis S, Spiliotis E, Assimakopoulos V. "Statistical and Machine Learning forecasting methods: Concerns and ways forward." PLoS ONE 2018;13(3):e0194889. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0194889.** Used 1045 monthly M3 series (81–126 months long), trained on n−18 observations and tested on the last 18. Eight statistical methods were compared with eight or more ML methods (MLP, BNN, RNN, LSTM, etc.). "The former [ML] are dominated across both accuracy measures used and for all forecasting horizons examined." "Even Naive 2 (a seasonal Random Walk benchmark) is more accurate than half of the ML methods." For one-step-ahead forecasts, the best MLP was only 0.19% sMAPE better than Naive 2 and about 1.24% worse than ARIMA. — [PMC5870978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5870978/)
  - On overfitting, LSTM had better in-sample fit but worse forecasts than simpler networks. MLP's fit error (2.11%) was lower than ARIMA's (2.59%), yet MLP forecast worse. "The old belief that minimizing the model fit errors would guarantee more accurate post-sample predictions does not hold." The authors attribute ARIMA's edge to AIC-penalised complexity. — [PMC5870978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5870978/)
  - Computational cost relative to naive: ETS about 34×, ARIMA about 44×. The authors caution that series length "may be a critical factor", and that much longer series may let ML "train their weights more optimally". — [PMC5870978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5870978/)
  - In the M3-based NN3 competition, "no ML method outperformed the Theta method". — [PMC5870978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5870978/)
- **M4: Makridakis S, Spiliotis E, Assimakopoulos V. "The M4 Competition: 100,000 time series and 61 forecasting methods." IJF 2020;36(1):54–74. doi:10.1016/j.ijforecast.2019.04.014.** The six pure ML methods "fared poorly", all falling below a benchmark combination of three simple statistical methods. Hybrids won: ES-RNN, and an ML-weighted combination of statistical methods. The results reaffirmed the value of combining. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169207019301128); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makridakis_Competitions)
- **M5: Makridakis S, Spiliotis E, Assimakopoulos V. "M5 accuracy competition: Results, findings, and conclusions." IJF 2022;38(4):1346–1364. doi:10.1016/j.ijforecast.2021.11.013.** 42,840 hierarchical Walmart series. LightGBM-based methods beat the best statistical benchmark (bottom-up exponential smoothing). It was the first M competition where the top methods were all pure ML, made possible by many long, related series and exogenous features (prices, promotions, calendar). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169207021001874)
- **Hyndman RJ & Athanasopoulos G, *Forecasting: Principles and Practice* (3rd ed., OTexts).** It defines the mean, naive ("all forecasts [are] the value of the last observation"), seasonal naive ("the last observed value from the same season") and drift methods. It states that "any forecasting methods we develop will be compared to these simple methods to ensure that the new method is better", and that sometimes a simple method is the best one available. — [FPP3 §5.2](https://otexts.com/fpp3/simple-methods.html). The book's time-series cross-validation (rolling forecast origin) section is at [otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html](https://otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html) (not re-fetched).
- **MASE** (Hyndman & Koehler 2006, IJF 22(4):679–688, doi:10.1016/j.ijforecast.2006.03.001) is scale-free. As Makridakis et al. 2018 describe it, its value is "less than one if the forecast is more accurate than the average model fitting prediction of the seasonal Naive benchmark, and greater than one if it is less accurate". — [PMC5870978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5870978/)
- Infectious-disease parallel: in the Dengue Forecasting Project, statistical models beat mechanistic ones and "overly complex forecasting models" had a downside — [PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/). Johansson 2016 warns that merging testing and validation produces "over-fitted models that perform poorly" prospectively — [PMC5036038](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5036038/)
- Forecastability: White & León (PLoS Comput Biol 2026, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014175) define forecastability via spectral entropy. It increases with population size (larger counts give smoother series), and forecast performance improves with forecastability. Some seasons and targets are "inherently more difficult to forecast". In the 2020 West Nile Virus challenge, skill was higher in counties with lower permutation entropy. — [PMC13102302](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13102302/)

### Inferences
- A typical dengue surveillance series (for example 10–20 years of weekly data for one city, so only 10–20 seasonal cycles) resembles the short M3 series where ML underperformed, not the M5 setting. Unless models pool many locations (global/cross-learning models) and are evaluated out of sample with rolling origins, complex ML is likely to overfit and fail to beat seasonal naive, SARIMA or persistence.
- Small, sparse, zero-heavy district series have low forecastability. Expect smaller gains over baselines there than at state or national level, consistent with Lopez 2024's finding that skill relative to baseline was higher for larger jurisdictions.

### Gaps
- Tashman LJ, "Out-of-sample tests of forecasting accuracy: an analysis and review", IJF 2000;16(4):437–450 (doi:10.1016/S0169-2070(00)00065-0) was not fetched, so its specific recommendations (rolling origin, multiple origins, avoiding in-sample selection) are not quoted here.
- I found no quantitative source giving a minimum number of seasons needed for dengue ML, beyond the 2019 PNAS use of 13 vs 23 seasons and Johansson 2016's 28-year split. The exact share of M5 teams beating the benchmark was not verified.

## Q5. Why 1–4-week forecasts lag peaks and behave like persistence

### Takeaway
Short-horizon models lean heavily on the latest observation (autoregressive inertia), and surveillance data are themselves delayed and backfilled. As a result, forecasts trail turning points: they undershoot during rapid growth and overshoot after peaks. Persistence baselines look good at peaks and plateaus but poor in growth and decline phases.

### Cited Findings
- COVID-19 cases: the baseline "performed relatively well in times of peaks … and worse … in increasing and decreasing phases because it forecasted a flat trend". Model skill was worst in increasing and then decreasing phases, and "forecasts did not anticipate the speed of increase in cases in early winter 2020." — [Lopez et al. 2024, PMC11098513](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11098513/)
- Germany and Poland: model inertia "can lead to delayed adaptation to changes and explains why numerous models—including the ensemble—showed overshoot … when cases started to plateau". In Poland, overshoot followed the case peak, and the 1-week-ahead median ensemble "only barely cover[ed] the next observed value". Growth-rate models "had a stronger tendency to overshoot", while human judgement did best right after trend changes. — [Bracher et al. 2021, PMC8397791](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8397791/)
- Dengue: "Week-to-week incidence varies substantially, making peaks hard to identify in real time". Skill rose around and after the observed peak and was lower for seasons with later and higher peaks. — [Johansson et al. 2019, PMC6883829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6883829/)
- Reporting artifacts: high baseline errors coincided with backfill outliers. — [Cramer et al. 2022, PMC9169655](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9169655/). Reporting delays also need explicit correction — [PLoS Comput Biol, "Addressing delayed case reporting in infectious disease forecast modeling"](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1010115) (not read in full)
- Dengue outbreak response: the lag from signal to response is usually more than 2 weeks and often much longer. Using the outbreak threshold as the alert means "late alert and delayed response". — [Badurdeen 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3697990/)

### Inferences
- A 4-week-ahead dengue model that leans on recent lags will look like a shifted copy of the observed curve. This yields good average error but little value for anticipating peaks. Evaluation should therefore include phase-specific scores (growth, peak, decline), peak-timing error and lead-time metrics, alongside overall MAE or WIS against persistence.

### Gaps
- No dengue-specific study was found that quantifies the lag (in weeks) between forecast peaks and observed peaks for 4-week-ahead models. I did not locate the Lutz et al. 2019 discussion of persistence-like behaviour in influenza forecasts (BMC Public Health, doi:10.1186/s12889-019-7966-8) beyond its title.
